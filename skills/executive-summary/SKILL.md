# Executive summary

The executive summary is the page a senior reader may read instead of the deck. It gives the answer first, then just enough support for the reader to agree, push back, or decide.

## 1. Know the reader and the decision

Note who will read it and what they must decide, if the user says. If not, write for a senior decision maker who will read only this page. Don't stop to ask; state your assumptions under "Check before you use it".

## 2. Write the governing thought

One sentence of about 30 words or fewer that answers the question the work set out to answer, phrased as a recommendation or a conclusion, with the most important number if there is one. "Moving elective surgery to a day-case unit frees 1,200 bed-days a year and clears the waiting list in 18 months" is a governing thought. "This document summarizes our findings" is not.

If the material doesn't support a firm answer, write the strongest honest one: what is known, what isn't, and how the next step settles it.

## 3. Choose the supporting points

- Three to five points, each one full sentence that could stand as a slide title.
- Mutually exclusive and together sufficient: no two points overlap, and the governing thought holds if the reader accepts all of them. Test each point by removing it. If the answer still stands without it, cut it or fold it into another point.
- Each point carries its key evidence: a number, a fact, or a short example from the user's material.
- Order the points deliberately: most important first, or in the order the logic runs (problem, cause, fix, payoff).
- Start each point with a short bold lead-in so the page can be skimmed.

If the reader lacks context, open with one or two sentences of situation and complication before the governing thought. Otherwise start with the answer.

## 4. End with the ask

Close with the decision needed, from whom, and by when, then the next steps with owners and dates where the material has them. Use placeholders for owners or dates you don't know.

## 5. Keep it honest and tight

- Use only numbers from the user's material, or simple arithmetic on them that you show. Where you need a number you don't have, write a placeholder in square brackets, such as [X%].
- Mark estimates, and add a source line.
- Stay under about 250 words unless the user asks for more.
- Plain words, active voice, no buzzwords. Numerals for data (12%, $4M, 31 hours); words for small counts in ordinary prose (two competitors).
- Never use em dashes or en dashes (Unicode U+2014 and U+2013) as punctuation. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses, or two sentences.

## Output format

For a deck, the default:

**Executive summary**

**<Governing thought, one sentence>**

1. **<Lead-in>:** <supporting point with evidence>
2. **<Lead-in>:** <supporting point with evidence>
3. **<Lead-in>:** <supporting point with evidence>

**Decision needed:** <decision, from whom, by when>

**Next steps:** <step, owner, date>

**Source:** <source line>

**Check before you use it:** <at most three one-line bullets: the assumptions and placeholders that matter most; leave this out if there are none>

For an email or memo, keep the same order in short paragraphs: the governing thought as the first sentence, one paragraph per supporting point with its bold lead-in, and the ask last.

## Final check

Read only the governing thought and the bold lead-ins. They should make the whole case on their own. Then check that nothing a skeptical reader would ask first (how much, how sure, what it costs, what could go wrong, what you need from them) is missing.
